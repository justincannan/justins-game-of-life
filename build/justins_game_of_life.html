Justin's Game of Life

Every living cell carries a compound, a product of primes, and its factorisation says what the cell is made of and what it can do: whether it moves, how hard it writes itself into its neighbours. Newborns copy a parent's compound, with errors, and are built from the molecules in the ground. Drag to pan, wheel or buttons to zoom; zoom in to read codes, out to see named forms.

Light lays down lamps that keep the light up, so a crowd can't starve there. Shade keeps light out: nothing lives or is born there. Wipe light removes both. Drawn light survives Reset.

Rates

Environment

Light and heat change in time and space. Choose a world: even light and no heat (the old world), a sunlit pond with day, night and drifting clouds, dark cold sea floor lit and warmed only by vents that pour out molecules, or hot springs on land. Water temperature matters to every cell: each compound has a preferred temperature (the prime 43 raises it), and cells away from it die more often and are born less often. Life also warms the water a little.

3D water: a grid of the chemistry squares across and several levels deep, run on the graphics card (WebGPU). Warm water rises through the levels, spreads at the surface and sinks where it is cool. Without a GPU, or while it starts, the two-layer model runs instead.

With two layers, warm water rises from the floor into the surface layer and spreads, and cool water sinks and returns along the floor; the cross-current is then unused, and the tide pushes both layers. Dormant cells can ride the rising water and settle far away.

Chaotic heat drives the ambient water temperature and every vent's strength from a Lorenz system (deterministic, never repeating), makes vents wander slowly, and sets off sudden eruptions. 0 gives the smooth cycles only.

Chemistry

The primes are the world's molecules. Each prime (2, 3, 5, 7 …) is a substance in the ground, rich in some places and poor in others. A cell's code is a compound, a product of primes, so its factorisation is its formula: Conway's Life, "born on 3, survive on 2 or 3", is 3 × 5³ = 375. To be born, a cell has to be built from the molecules at that spot, one for each prime in its compound. When a cell dies its molecules are set free again.

Defence against invaders. A cell counts neighbours of its own lineage in full and strangers at a fraction; a lineage marks the ground it fills, so foreign births are less likely there; and births into open ground cost more molecules.

What each prime does

Signalling

Living cells give off a signal into the ground. It spreads a little and fades, so every place holds a smoothed measure of how crowded it is. The quorum gene (the prime 59) lets a cell read it: where the signal is at or above the quorum level, the cell stops giving birth into squares with few neighbours (a dormant cell turns into a creeper) and spends less light. One cell can therefore act as an explorer at the edge and a settler inside the crowd. The gene starts absent and appears by mutation. Show the signal from the molecule menu.

Roles, sex and predation

Three more genes in the extended genome. Like the quorum gene they start absent and appear by mutation; each strength 1 to 3 costs a little light to carry.

Producers (a division of labour): in a crowd, a producer secretes the molecules of its own compound into the ground and pays for it in light. The crowd's builders are fed by it, so it is a gift to whoever lives nearby, kin or not.
Recombination raises the chance that two different parents mix their codes. Predators eat a neighbour of another lineage, gaining its light and freeing its molecules.

Energy molecule

Living cells leak a little energy into the water and dead cells set free what they held. It spreads, rides the current, and slowly decays. A heterotroph gene (the prime 79, strength 1 to 3) lets a cell that has run short of light take the energy up from the water instead, but it keeps only part of what it takes; the rest is lost, which is why each step along a food chain passes on less. Show it with Show: energy molecule.

Sticking together

The adhesion gene (the prime 83, strength 1 to 3) makes the cells of a lineage cling. A free form whose cells carry it can become a clump, and the stronger the gene, the likelier. A cell born beside a clump's cell is born into that clump, so a clump that sticks goes on as one unit, pooling light inside. Carrying the gene costs a little light. The cells of a clump are held in a shared slime, so a gap of a cell or two does not split it. How readily clumps form, and what they cost to keep, are set by Clumps forming and Clump upkeep above. Outline them with Show clumps at the top of the world.

Line of expression

Each cell carries an ordered sequence of monomers, the 16 molecules, and its compound (its rule) is what the sequence infolds into. Read along the sequence, a monomer that meets its complement, the molecule eight places away, pairs with it, and both are buried; the monomers left unpaired are exposed, and their counts make the compound. A child copies its parent's sequence, or crosses two parents' sequences, with copying errors; now and then a stretch is copied twice, leaving the second copy free to vary, or a stretch is lost. Contagion splices a stretch of one cell's sequence into its neighbour's. A longer sequence costs more light to copy. Many sequences infold into the same compound, so many copying errors change nothing. Click a cell to see its sequence and how it infolds.

Infolded pairs at work

The monomers a sequence leaves exposed make its compound, its rule; the pairs it buries do work. A pair of a monomer with its complement is an enzyme of one of eight kinds, by which pair it is: light harvest (lower upkeep), respiration (more from sugar), uptake (fewer molecules to build a child), keeping water, heat tolerance, copying fidelity (fewer copying errors), defence (bites turned away) and building (cheaper births), each up to strength 3. Every monomer that pairs to work no longer counts toward the rule, so a sequence trades rule against work.

Soma and germ

A clump's code, its nucleus, can set its cells to different work. Where the code calls for it, the clump's innermost cells become soma: they do not breed, and they pass half their spare light to the outer cells, the germ line, which breed into the open ground around the clump. How many cells serve (none, 20, 40 or 60%) is written in the code and mutates when the clump divides or buds, so clumps can evolve toward working insides or away from them.

The nucleus can also regulate what its soma cells express. Where its code says so, it silences one kind of monomer in them: they infold without it, so their rule and their enzymes change while they serve. Their sequence is untouched, so a cell that leaves the clump, and every child, expresses all of it again.

Dormant cells

A cell with the dormant cell-forming gene (the prime 89, strength 1 to 3) that starves or whose ground dries out may turn into a dormant cell: a dormant cell that pays almost nothing to live, cannot give birth, stands drying and heat far better, and is carried by the current even out of a crowd. A dormant cell wakes when light and water come back. Show them with View: Dormant cells; in the normal view they are pale.

Reactions

Switch on real reactions in place of the bare energy bookkeeping. The water holds carbon dioxide, oxygen and water itself, and the energy molecule becomes sugar. When a cell builds a child or leaks sugar it fixes carbon: CO₂ + water + light → sugar + O₂. A heterotroph, a predator or rot burns sugar: with oxygen, sugar + O₂ → CO₂ + water at full yield; without it, it ferments, sugar → CO₂, at a lower yield. The world starts with no oxygen, so the first consumers ferment. Every child takes a little water and every death gives it back; water also carries the molecules, so dry ground slows them. Heat dries the water and rain refills it; a cell in dry ground may dry out and die, cells at an edge sooner, and a clump shelters its cells. Vents breathe out CO₂. A little sugar is buried in the mud for good, leaving its oxygen behind; iron in the ground rusts and soaks oxygen up until it runs out, so oxygen can rise only slowly, as it did on the early Earth.
